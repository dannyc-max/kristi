Dye Notes

Dye Notes

Here's exactly how I dye my cloth: what I use, how much, and what goes wrong. No secret recipes. If you want to try it yourself, everything you need is on this page.

I dye cotton double gauze and linen at home, mostly in my washing machine and in buckets. I write down every batch: the dye, the water, the weight of the fabric, and what actually came out. Sometimes a mistake turns into a new color.

What I use

The kit

Dharma Fiber Reactive Procion MX dyePowdered dye for cotton, linen and other plant fibers. It bonds with the fiber, so the color doesn't wash out.
Soda ashThe fixer. It raises the pH so the dye bonds to the cloth.
SynthrapolA detergent for the final wash. It carries loose dye away so it doesn't settle back on the fabric.
A washing machineFor solid colors on big pieces like throws and baby blankets.
BucketsFor low water immersion and shibori, where I want mottled or patterned color.
A kitchen scale and measuring spoonsUsed only for dye, never for food again.
Cotton double gauze and linenPlant fibers take fiber reactive dye best.
A notebookThe most important tool on this list. Every batch gets written down.

Three ways I dye

Three methods

[PHOTO: Washer dyeing]

Washing machine

The machine does the stirring, so the color comes out even. This is how I dye the solid baby blankets and throws.

LOOK
Smooth, solid color
GOOD FOR
Big pieces, matching sets
WATCH OUT
All that water dilutes the dye. Colors can come out much lighter than the chart.
[PHOTO: Low water immersion]

Low water immersion

Fabric is scrunched into a bucket with just enough water to cover it. The dye can't move evenly, so it pools and leaves a mottled, painterly color.

LOOK
Mottled, deep, no two alike
GOOD FOR
Landscape skies and ridges, fixing a color that came out wrong
WATCH OUT
Less water makes the color strong. Measure carefully.
[PHOTO: Shibori]

Shibori

Folding, twisting, binding or clamping the cloth before it goes in the dye, so some parts resist the color. I learned it in classes at Wax On Boutique in Asheville.

LOOK
Patterns: stripes, rings, grids
GOOD FOR
Shibori throws, purses
WATCH OUT
You don't really know what you got until you untie it. That's the fun part.

From the journal

I asked for truffle and got rose. Then cherry.

I wanted a truffle brown. I measured the dye exactly the way the ratio says for the weight of my fabric, set the washing machine going, and waited.

It came out light rose. Pretty, but not even close to truffle.

Here's what I think happened: a washing machine uses a lot of water. The ratio was right for the fabric, but the dye was spread through so much water that only a little of it made it into the cloth. That gave me a pale, pinky version of the color.

So I over-dyed it with low water immersion: the same rose fabric, scrunched in a bucket with much less water, and a heavier hand with the dye.

1 tablespoon + 1 teaspoon of truffle. Dark cherry.

Still not truffle! But it's a deep, rich cherry, and I can get it again because I wrote it down.

That's the point of the dye log. Every batch goes in the notebook, mistakes included, because the mistakes are where the new colors come from. Next up, I'm going the other way: one teaspoon in three gallons of water, with the fabric weighed to match. Pictures when it's done.

Good to know

Safety & care

IF YOU TRY THIS AT HOME

  • Wear a dust mask and gloves when you measure dye powder. Fine powder is easy to breathe in.
  • Keep dye tools separate from kitchen tools, for good.
  • Soda ash is caustic, so gloves on for that too.
  • Run an empty hot wash after dyeing in the washing machine.
  • Write everything down, including the batches that go wrong.

CARING FOR HAND-DYED PIECES

  • Every piece is washed out with Synthrapol before it leaves the house.
  • Wash with like colors in cold or warm water.
  • Hand-dyed color can shift a little in the first wash. That's normal.
  • Double gauze gets softer every time you wash it.
